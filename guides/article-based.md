# Article Based

**Guide id:** `article-based`
**Use for:** any Upwork job, when you want the short, human, client-first cover letter that
Upwork's own guidance and working freelancers describe, rather than a long structured pitch.

This guide is built from four sources, read 2026-09-07:

- Upwork, "How To Create a Proposal On Upwork That Wins Jobs" (Cassie Moorhead)
- Upwork, "Upwork Cover Letter Tips: 2026 Guide With Samples and Proposal Examples" (May 15, 2026)
- Medium / ILLUMINATION, "5 Tips to Win Proposals and Get Clients on Upwork" (Abhi Thakur)
- LinkedIn, "Upwork Proposals: Tips and Tricks" (Alexandra Mosnitska, Mar 20 2021)

The person profile (`Mario.md`, `Zachary.md`, ...) supplies every fact. This file supplies the
shape. Where the sources disagree with the workspace's older structured style, this guide follows
the sources.

## The flow, start to finish

All four sources describe the same sequence, and only the middle third is writing. Work it in this
order.

**Qualify the job, before spending a Connect**

1. **Check the proposal count.** Over 20, think twice. Over 50, skip it: they're either
   interviewing already, or they just turned down fifty people.
2. **Check the client.** Payment verified, hiring history, and the reviews other freelancers left
   about them.
3. **Decide it's a real fit.** Bid narrow, not wide. The Medium author lost 40 Connects bidding on
   everything before he started winning by bidding only on work he could actually do.

**Read the post like a document, not a summary**

4. **Look for planted instructions.** Some clients ask you to open with a specific word to prove
   you read it. Missing that is an instant no.
5. **Note the screening questions.** Your answers appear to the client *above* the cover letter, so
   they're read first. Treat them as the more important half.
6. **Find the client's name.** It's usually in the feedback they left previous freelancers. Five
   seconds, and it separates you from every templated proposal in the list.

**Write the letter, five moves**

7. **Greet them by name.** "Hi Rebecca," never "To Whom It May Concern."
8. **Open on their problem.** Restate the core issue or name something specific from the post. This
   does double duty: it proves you read the post, and it's the only part visible in the results
   list before the client decides whether to click.
9. **One closest proof.** The single nearest thing you've done, with a real number if the profile
   has one. Not a portfolio tour.
10. **Briefly, how you'd approach theirs.** Enough that they can see you know the route, not a
    step-by-step plan.
11. **A specific call to action.** A call, a walkthrough, or a short paid test.

**Check it, then let it go**

12. **Proofread, and read it aloud once.**
13. **Answer the screening questions properly**, not as an afterthought.
14. **Boost it** if it's a genuinely strong match and you want the visibility.
15. **Send it and forget it.** Don't refresh. Write the next one.

Two constraints wrap the whole thing: **200 to 300 words**, and **only your opening lines are
visible** until the client clicks.

The sections below expand each step. This one is the order.

## The one rule underneath all of it

**Write about their project, not about yourself.** Every source says this, and the Medium article
shows it best with the author's own before and after. The weak version led with "having been in
this writing business for three years, I have written dozens of articles for various clients."
The version that got replies led with "I see you need a writer for a PC blog," then said he had
written in that exact niche, then offered samples. Same person, same experience, opposite result.

Alexandra Mosnitska puts the same point as: say why you're interested, what similar work you've
done, and how you can contribute. Not paragraphs about what a great expert you are.

## Length and format

- **200 to 300 words or fewer.** Upwork's 2026 cover letter guide states this outright. Three
  short paragraphs is the stated sweet spot.
- **Only the opening lines appear in the client's results list.** Everything else is invisible
  until they click. The first two sentences carry the entire click decision.
- Short paragraphs. Bullets where a list is genuinely a list, especially for a stack or a
  deliverable set. Not for prose.
- **Bold two or three key phrases, and no more.** Not headers, and never one per paragraph as a
  rule. Pick the phrases that stop a skimming eye: the line that reframes their problem, the hard
  requirement you meet, the credibility claim. Upwork's guidance is emphasis "to draw attention to
  key points," which is what this is. Headers on a 250 word letter make it look like a filled-in
  template, and templated is the one thing every source warns against.
- Remember bold on Upwork means Unicode characters, not markup. They don't render everywhere and
  screen readers read them out character by character, so each one has to earn its place.
- An occasional emoji is allowed if it suits the tone and the field. Don't decorate.
- Proofread it. Read it aloud once.

## Structure

Upwork lists ten possible components: summary, problem statement, proposed solution, benefits,
timeline, costs, credentials, testimonials or case studies, terms, and a call to action. **You do
not use all ten.** At 250 words you pick the four or five that this job actually needs. In
practice:

1. **Greeting with their name.** "Hi Rebecca," not "To Whom It May Concern." Both Upwork and
   Mosnitska give the same trick for finding it: read the feedback other freelancers left on the
   client's profile, which usually names them. It takes five seconds and it separates you from
   every templated proposal in the list.
2. **Their problem, in your first sentence.** Restate the core problem or name something specific
   from the post. This is the proof you read it, and it's the only part most clients see.
3. **The nearest thing you've actually done**, with the result if there is one. One example, the
   closest one, not a tour of the portfolio.
4. **How you'd approach theirs**, briefly. Not a step-by-step plan, just enough that they can see
   you know the route.
5. **A specific call to action.** A call, a walkthrough, or a short paid test.

## The greeting

Quoting the Upwork cover letter guide directly: "Start your cover letter with a friendly,
professional greeting. If the client's name is available, use it. If not, a simple 'Hi' or 'Hello
there' works well. It's best to avoid outdated phrases like 'To Whom It May Concern.'"

The greetings actually used across the four sources: `Hi Rebecca,` `Hi Riley,` `Hi Jordan,`
`Hello Nicki,` `Hi there,` `Hi,` `Hello,` and Mosnitska's `Hey John!`.

**Do not open with `Hi` and a wave emoji.** That opener belongs to the structured style and appears
nowhere in these sources. On emoji generally, the one mention in the sources is that you "might
consider using an occasional emoji to draw attention to key points," if it fits the tone. That is
an occasional in-body device, not a fixed opener.

## Sound like a person

Mosnitska's framing: the emotional connection matters more in freelance than in-house. Clients
sometimes hire the person they enjoyed talking to over the stronger CV. They are not your boss,
so don't write like a servant. Drop "Dear Sir/Madam."

Upwork's version of the same idea, as a contrast pair:

- Generic: "I'm an accomplished copywriter with 10 years of experience writing sales copy."
- Memorable: "I use my obsession with words to create punchy sales copy for female-owned
  businesses. This has led to a 32% increase in sales for one of my clients."

Personality is not padding. It's the thing that makes one of forty proposals memorable.

**The modern version of this problem is sounding AI-written.** The specific tells: "not X, but Y"
and "rather than" constructions stacked up, paragraphs ending on neat aphorisms, everything in
threes, and every single sentence doing work with no aside or reaction anywhere. Cap the
antithesis constructions at one, end on the plain point rather than the clever one, and put one
genuine reaction to their post in your own words. Let a sentence be short and flat. Allow one
honest reservation. The full list is in `guides/brainstormed.md` under "Sound like a person" and
applies here unchanged.

## Kill the buzzwords

"Motivated," "team player," "detail-oriented," "results-driven," "passionate." They cost words and
say nothing. Replace each with the specific thing that would have made you use the word.

- Weak: "I am a motivated team player."
- Strong: "I managed a team of five salespeople. Together we beat our annual target by 15%."
- Weak: "Created a website that increased sales."
- Strong: "Built a responsive, conversion-optimized site that lifted web sales 20% and mobile-web
  sales 45%."

**Only use numbers that exist in the person profile.** These examples are the sources' own. If the
profile has no metric for a project, describe the scope and the system instead, and never invent a
percentage to fill the slot.

## Follow the job post exactly (steps 4 to 6, expanded)

- **Answer the screening questions.** Mosnitska notes they appear to the client *above* your cover
  letter, so they are read first. Treat them as the more important half.
- **Some clients plant an instruction** to check you're paying attention, like a word to open with.
  Missing it is an instant no.
- If the post says skip the generic cover letter and answer two questions, do exactly that, even
  where it conflicts with the shape above.

## Close with a next step (step 11, expanded)

Never end on "Thanks for your time." Upwork's suggested closes:

- "I'd be happy to set up a quick call to learn more about your goals and walk you through how I'd
  approach the project."
- "Let me know if you'd like to set up a quick paid test, happy to show what I can do first."
- "I'd love to hear more about the scope and timeline, just let me know when you're free."

Polite, confident, forward-moving, and clear about what happens next.

## Evidence and links

- Include one or two links to genuinely relevant work, not a catalogue. Mosnitska's rule is fewer
  samples, chosen as the closest match to this client's project, rather than variety.
- Talent with a published portfolio are hired **9x more often** (Upwork).
- Testimonials from before Upwork can be pasted in, and Upwork supports requesting them.
- Same hard rule as always: **never invent a URL, a client name, or a metric.** Use only what the
  person profile contains. Where the profile records no portfolio, lead with scope and system
  detail instead.

## Bidding judgement (steps 1 to 3, expanded)

From Mosnitska and the Medium piece, both worth applying before spending Connects:

- **Check the proposal count.** Over 20, think twice. Over 50, usually skip: they're likely
  interviewing already, and if they aren't, they rejected fifty people.
- **Read the client's history** and payment verification.
- **Bid narrow, not wide.** The Medium author lost 40 Connects bidding on everything and started
  winning once he bid only on work he could actually do.
- **Don't compete on price.** Someone will always be cheaper. The stated budget is often a
  placeholder from a client who doesn't know what the work costs, and it is negotiable at
  interview.
- **Send it and forget it.** Don't refresh. Write the next one.

## Delivery format

**None of this section comes from the four articles.** These are this workspace's mechanics, the
same in every guide, so the output stays paste-ready and the UI keeps working. Everything
above this heading is drawn from the sources.

- **Reply with the proposal and nothing else.** No preamble, no analysis afterward. Commentary only
  if asked.
- Output it in a single fenced code block, with markdown bold converted to Unicode sans-serif bold
  (capitals from U+1D5D4, lowercase from U+1D5EE, digits from U+1D7EC), because Upwork renders
  markdown as literal asterisks.
- **Write no files.** The proposal lives in the reply.
- **No images by default.** The sources never mention them. Attach one
  only if the client asks for a plan or a timeline and it genuinely helps.
- Sign off with the name from the person profile.
- Use contractions throughout. No em dashes.
